2.2 Potential Energy & Bond Strength

Reading a PE-vs-distance curve

  • x-position of the minimum = bond length (most stable separation: attraction and repulsion balanced).
  • Depth below zero = bond energy (energy released forming the bond = energy absorbed to break it; always report as positive kJ/mol).
  • Far apart → PE ≈ 0, no bond. Too close → repulsions (nucleus–nucleus and electron–electron) win, PE shoots up.
  • At the minimum, attractions (nucleus of one atom ↔ electrons of the other) and repulsions are balanced.

Predicting how two curves compare

  • Bigger atoms (down a group) → longer bond (minimum shifts right) and weaker bond (shallower well). Cl–Cl shorter/stronger than Br–Br, which is shorter/stronger than I–I. (Small exceptions exist, e.g. F–F, but follow the trend unless data say otherwise.)
  • Higher bond order → shorter and stronger (minimum left and deeper). Example data: C–C 154 pm, 348 kJ/mol; C=C 134 pm, 614; C≡C 120 pm, 839. Bond order = shared pairs.
  • Curve-drawing check: shorter bond ⇒ minimum further left; stronger ⇒ minimum lower. A curve that is further left and shallower (or right and deeper) for the same bond type is internally inconsistent — a classic "find the error" item.

Lattice energy (ionic strength)

Lattice energy = energy needed to separate 1 mol of solid ionic compound into gaseous ions (e.g. NaCl(s) → Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g), 788 kJ/mol). Coulomb: F ∝ (q₁q₂)/r². So lattice energy rises with larger ion charges and smaller ionic radii (shorter distance between nuclei). Charge usually matters more: MgO (2+/2−) ≫ NaF (1+/1−).

Ranking recipe: 1) compare the product of charges; 2) if tied, compare ion sizes (same group: higher up = smaller ion = bigger lattice energy; e.g. LiF > NaF > NaCl > NaBr). Higher lattice energy → higher melting point and harder crystal.

Practice Problems

Try each before opening the answer.

1. Curve A has its minimum at 150 pm and −300 kJ/mol. Curve B has its minimum at 190 pm and −200 kJ/mol. Which is the shorter bond? The stronger? If one is C=C and the other C–C, which is which?
A is shorter and stronger. A = C=C (higher order → shorter, stronger); B = C–C.
2. Rank by increasing bond length and by increasing bond energy: C≡N, C=N, C–N.
Length: C≡N < C=N < C–N. Energy: C–N < C=N < C≡N (the order flips).
3. Rank by lattice energy: KBr, NaCl, MgS.
KBr < NaCl < MgS. MgS has 2+/2− (4× the charge product). Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are smaller than K⁺ and Br⁻ → shorter distance → greater energy.
4. Predict whether the lattice energy of SrO is greater or less than that of CaO.
Less. Same charges, but Sr²⁺ is larger than Ca²⁺ (more shells), so ion separation is greater and attraction weaker.
5. Is H–I longer or shorter than H–Br, and which has the larger bond energy?
H–I is longer (I is larger) and has the smaller bond energy; a longer bond is easier to break.
6. Why does PE rise very steeply when two atoms are pushed closer than the bond length?
Nucleus–nucleus and electron–electron repulsions grow faster than the attractions as the atoms overlap.

FRQ Practice

Write a full response first, then check the scoring points.

Cl–Cl: 199 pm, 243 kJ/mol. I–I: 266 pm, 151 kJ/mol. (a) Predict whether Br–Br is longer or shorter than Cl–Cl and its energy relative to I–I. (b) Justify. (c) A student draws the I–I curve deeper than Cl–Cl. Identify the error. (d) Predict whether NaI or KI has the larger lattice energy.
(a) Br–Br: longer than Cl–Cl and shorter than I–I; energy between 151 and 243. (b) Br is larger than Cl, smaller than I; longer bond → weaker. (c) I–I should be shallower (smaller energy) and its minimum further right. (d) NaI: Na⁺ smaller than K⁺ → shorter distance → greater attraction (same charges).

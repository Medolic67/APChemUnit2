2.1 Types of Chemical Bonds

Electronegativity (EN) trends

EN = an atom's pull on shared bonding electrons. It increases left→right across a period (more protons, same shell → greater effective nuclear charge, smaller radius) and decreases down a group (more shells → bonding electrons farther from nucleus and shielded). F is highest (3.98); Cs/Fr lowest. Noble gases are generally left out.

H 2.20Li 0.98Be 1.57B 2.04C 2.55N 3.04O 3.50F 3.98
—Na 0.93Mg 1.31Al 1.61Si 1.90P 2.19S 2.58Cl 3.16
—K 0.82Ca 1.00Ga 1.81Ge 2.01As 2.18Se 2.55Br 2.96

Notice C–H (ΔEN 0.35) is treated as effectively nonpolar, and P–H is essentially zero.

Three bond types, by who holds the electrons

  • Nonpolar covalent: equal or near-equal sharing (same element, or ΔEN very small).
  • Polar covalent: unequal sharing. Higher-EN atom gets δ−, lower-EN atom gets δ+. A bond dipole arrow points toward the δ− (more electronegative) atom; bigger ΔEN → bigger dipole moment (longer arrow).
  • Ionic: electrons essentially transferred; ions held by electrostatic attraction. Metallic: delocalized valence electrons shared among metal cations.
Quick rule (classroom guideline, not a law): ΔEN above ~1.7 → ionic; ~0.5–1.6 → polar covalent; below ~0.4–0.5 → nonpolar covalent. Metal + nonmetal → ionic; two nonmetals → covalent; metal + metal → metallic. The CED says it plainly: all polar bonds have some ionic character, and ionic vs. covalent is a continuum.

Ranking "ionic character" or polarity

Compute ΔEN for every bond (or compare positions on the table: farther apart = more ionic). For compounds with the same anion, a metal further left/lower has lower EN → more ionic character (e.g. Cs–Cl > Na–Cl > Al–Cl > P–Cl). Metal–nonmetal pairs beat nonmetal–nonmetal pairs; two identical atoms (Cl–Cl) are zero. Transition metals like Zn, Ag, Cu have higher EN than s-block metals, so ZnCl₂ is less ionic than a calcium or strontium salt.

When "metal + nonmetal" fails: the data decide. SnCl₂ (white crystalline solid, mp 247 °C) behaves ionically; SnCl₄ (colorless liquid, mp −34 °C) behaves molecularly. Same two elements, different bonding — because a higher-charged Sn⁴⁺ pulls electron density toward itself and the bond becomes more covalent. Read the melting point, state, and conductivity data first.

Related Unit 1 ideas used here

  • Law of definite proportions / empirical formula: elemental mass ratio in a pure compound is fixed. Empirical formula = lowest whole-number atom ratio. Method: assume 100 g → grams to moles (÷ molar mass) → divide all by the smallest → scale to whole numbers (×2 if .5, ×3 if .33/.67). Example: 52.9 % Al, 47.1 % O → 1.96 mol Al, 2.94 mol O → 1 : 1.5 → ×2 → Al₂O₃. Percent composition = (mass of element in 1 mol ÷ molar mass) × 100.
  • Ion charges from position: Group 1 → +1, 2 → +2, 13 → +3, 15 → 3−, 16 → 2−, 17 → 1−. Elements in a column form analogous compounds (NaCl ≈ KCl; CaO ≈ SrO). Ionic formulas are charge-neutral: Mg²⁺ + Cl⁻ → MgCl₂. Cations are smaller than their atoms; anions are larger.

Practice Problems

Try each before opening the answer.

1. Rank in order of increasing polarity: C–H, O–H, N–H, H–F.
ΔEN: C–H 0.35, N–H 0.84, O–H 1.30, H–F 1.78 → C–H < N–H < O–H < H–F.
2. For S–Cl and B–Cl, put δ+/δ− and give the dipole direction. Which is more polar?
Cl (3.16) beats S (2.58) and B (2.04): Cl is δ−, dipole points toward Cl in both. ΔEN 0.58 vs 1.12, so B–Cl is more polar.
3. Which has the most ionic character: LiI, NaI, KI? Justify with a periodic trend.
KI. K is lowest in the group so it has the lowest EN (larger radius, outer e⁻ farther from nucleus, more shielding). Largest ΔEN with I → most ionic.
4. A white solid melts at 85 °C and does not conduct electricity as a solid, liquid, or in water. Another melts at 801 °C and conducts only when molten or dissolved. Classify each.
First: molecular covalent (low mp, no ions/mobile e⁻). Second: ionic (high mp; ions free only when molten/aqueous).
5. A compound is 85.6 % C and 14.4 % H by mass. Find its empirical formula.
Per 100 g: C 85.6/12.01 = 7.13 mol; H 14.4/1.008 = 14.3 mol. Ratio 1 : 2 → CH₂.
6. Explain why ΔEN alone cannot decide whether a bond is ionic or covalent.
Bonding is a continuum; all polar bonds have some ionic character. The metal/nonmetal types and measured properties (mp, conductivity) are the better evidence, e.g. SnCl₄ is a molecular liquid despite Sn being a metal.

FRQ Practice

Write a full response first, then check the scoring points.

A student studies PCl₃, KCl, and Cu. (a) Classify the bonding in each. (b) Which conducts electricity as a solid, and which only when molten? (c) Compare the polarity of P–Cl and S–Cl using periodic trends.
(a) PCl₃ polar covalent (molecular); KCl ionic; Cu metallic. (b) Cu conducts as a solid (delocalized e⁻); KCl only molten/aqueous (mobile ions); PCl₃ does not conduct. (c) Cl–S ΔEN 0.58 vs Cl–P 0.97 (P 2.19): S is closer to Cl on the table, so S–Cl is less polar; in both, Cl is δ−.

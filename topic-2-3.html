Lattices, particle diagrams, conductivity, brittleness

2.3 Structure of Ionic Solids

  • Ions sit in a repeating 3-D array maximizing attractions, minimizing repulsions: every cation is surrounded by anions and vice-versa. Particle diagrams should show alternating charges, ions touching, all the same size per ion type.
  • Particle-diagram checklist (the usual "find the errors"): (1) correct charges (Mg²⁺ not Mg⁺; Na⁺ not Na²⁺); (2) correct ratio matching the formula and neutral overall (MgCl₂ has twice as many Cl⁻ as Mg²⁺); (3) cations drawn smaller than anions (and a 2+ ion is smaller than a 1+ ion of similar period); (4) like charges not clumped together; (5) ions drawn as an ordered lattice, not isolated molecules or a random pile; (6) no shared-electron "molecule" bonds.
  • Conductivity: solid → ions locked in place, no mobile charge carriers, no conduction. Molten or dissolved → ions free to move → conducts. Two ways to make NaCl conduct: melt it or dissolve it in water.
  • Brittleness: a stress shifts one layer by one ion. Now like charges (+/+ and −/−) line up, repulsion splits the crystal along a plane → cleavage/shatter. Contrast with metals, where shifting layers keeps the same environment.

Practice Problems

Try each before opening the answer.

1. Describe a correct particle diagram for Na₂O.
Ordered lattice of alternating ions with 2 Na⁺ for every 1 O²⁻, charges labeled, and the Na⁺ ions drawn smaller than the O²⁻ ions.
2. A diagram of CaCl₂ shows equal numbers of Ca⁺ and Cl⁻ in a random pile. List the errors.
Ca should be Ca²⁺; ratio should be 1 Ca²⁺ : 2 Cl⁻ (neutral overall); not an ordered lattice; Ca²⁺ should be drawn smaller than Cl⁻.
3. Explain why KBr(s) does not conduct but KBr(l) does.
In the solid, ions are fixed in the lattice; no mobile charge carriers. Melting frees the ions to move and carry charge.
4. Explain at the particle level why a hammer blow shatters a salt crystal.
The blow shifts a layer by one ion so like charges line up. The repulsion splits the crystal along the plane.
5. Which has the higher melting point, NaCl or MgO? Why?
MgO. 2+/2− ions and smaller separation → much stronger attractions (Coulomb's law) → higher lattice energy.

FRQ Practice

Write a full response first, then check the scoring points.

A diagram of LiBr(s) shows Li⁺ and Br⁻ the same size, clumped in groups of like charge. (a) Identify two errors and fix them. (b) Explain why LiBr conducts when dissolved but not as a solid. (c) Predict whether LiBr or LiF has the higher melting point.
(a) Li⁺ must be smaller than Br⁻; ions must alternate in an ordered 3-D lattice. (b) Dissolving frees ions to move. (c) LiF: F⁻ is smaller than Br⁻, so attractions are stronger.
